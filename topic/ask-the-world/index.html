        🇧🇷How does Brazil mark Cross-border Q&A?

        When you ask about everyday life in Brazil, the layer most easily overlooked is that holidays come at federal, state and municipal levels: federal ones are written into Law 662, while religious holidays are declared by municipal law and capped at four. So the same date can be a day off in one municipality and a working day in the one next door. The other thing is proof of residence — Brazil has no household register, and almost any errand requires a recent utility bill, which is where newcomers most often get stuck. When asking about a holiday it is worth asking which state and which municipality, since the same date can be a day off in one town and a working day next door.

        🇨🇳How does China mark Cross-border Q&A?

        When you ask about everyday life in mainland China, the two layers most easily missed are shifted workdays and the household registration. The statutory number of days off is not the same thing as the actual length of the break, which is decided each year by a State Council notice; and school admission, home purchase and where social insurance is handled all follow the hukou, so 'where do you live' and 'where is your hukou' can have two different answers. Asking which layer the question is about, before answering, saves a great deal of confusion. It also helps to separate days off for all citizens from days off for part of them: International Women's Day, Youth Day and Children's Day belong to the latter.

        🇮🇩How does Indonesia mark Cross-border Q&A?

        When you ask about everyday life in Indonesia, it helps to separate national holidays from joint leave: the first is written into regulation, the second is supplementary leave arranged each year by a joint decree of three ministries, and only the two together give the actual length of a break. Another common gap is that the address on someone's identity card is not necessarily where they live, which affects the polling station, the school catchment and where many procedures are handled. Spelling out both distinctions is what makes the information accurate. When asking 'when', it helps to specify Islamic, Gregorian or Balinese, since three calendars coexist on the same holiday list.

        🇮🇳How does India mark Cross-border Q&A?

        When you ask about everyday life in India, the first thing to establish is which state and which community. The national holiday list is short — 1 May is not on it, and neither is Easter Sunday; marriage runs through personal law alongside the Special Marriage Act; the school year starts anywhere between March and June depending on the state. So a useful answer usually carries its qualifiers rather than offering one nationally valid statement — that kind of statement is usually wrong about India. The same festival can carry a completely different name in another state, so asking with the local name usually gets a more accurate answer than the English one.

        🇯🇵How does Japan mark Cross-border Q&A?

        When you ask about everyday life in Japan, the timeline is largely set by two hinges: April, and the turn of the year. School year, fiscal year and staff transfers all align on 1 April, while the new-year period is the heaviest homecoming season of the year. The other thing frequently misread is that 'not a holiday' does not mean 'not important' — 1 May, Christmas and Mother's Day are none of them public holidays, yet participation is high. A useful answer states both whether the day is a statutory holiday and whether people actually do the thing. When asking about a date it is worth also asking which year — the holiday law has been amended several times, and outdated information stays online for a long while.

        🇹🇼How does Taiwan mark Cross-border Q&A?

        When you ask about everyday life in Taiwan, the answers most often trip over the gap between the statutory date and what people actually do. The school year opens on 1 August but classes start in early September; Mother's Day and Father's Day are named in the statute and explicitly given no day off; when Children's Day collides with Tomb-Sweeping Day there is a dedicated substitution rule. So the answer to 'when do you have holidays' is frequently not the answer to 'when are you actually celebrating' — separating those two layers is what makes a reply usable. Another thing often missed is whether a date is lunar or Gregorian — the same festival name can sit a month apart depending on which calendar is meant.

        🇺🇸How does United States mark Cross-border Q&A?

        When you ask about everyday life in the United States, the commonest mistake is assuming a national standard exists. School years are set by districts, marriage by states, addresses need no registration, and most religious observances are not federal holidays — there are in fact very few federal-level rules. So the correct answer to 'how does the United States do it' usually begins with a question back: which state, which district. What can be answered nationally often comes down to eleven federal holidays and a handful of constitutional amendments. If someone gives a single nationwide figure, it is usually a federal holiday or a constitutional-level rule; everything else has to be chased downward.
