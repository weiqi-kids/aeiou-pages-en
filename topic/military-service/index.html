Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper

🌎How the world celebrates ↓

Article 1 of Taiwan's Military Service Act is a single sentence — every male citizen has the duty to serve — and Article 16 sets active conscript service at one year. China's constitution calls military service an "honourable obligation". Brazil's constitution says obligatory, then lets conscientious objectors take alternative service and exempts women and clergy in peacetime. The United States is stranger: 50 U.S.C. §3802 requires men aged 18 to 26 to register, and almost nobody is ever called. Japan's constitution keeps no land, sea or air forces at all. Indonesia and India both write a duty to defend into their constitutions and have no conscription. The same phrase, and a gap that runs from a year in barracks to a single form.

        🇧🇷How does Brazil mark Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper?

        Article 143 of Brazil's constitution opens briefly: military service is obligatory under the terms of the law. The two paragraphs that follow are where the substance is. Paragraph 1 requires the armed forces to assign alternative service to those who, in peacetime and after enlisting, invoke an imperative of conscience — and the constitution itself defines what that means: one arising from religious belief or from philosophical or political conviction. Writing conscientious objection into the constitutional text, definition included, is something only Brazil does among these seven. Paragraph 2 exempts women and clergy from compulsory service in peacetime, though they remain subject to other charges. The time frame is in Article 5 of Law 4,375 of 1964: in peacetime the obligation begins on 1 January of the year the citizen turns eighteen and runs to 31 December of the year he turns forty-five; a further paragraph allows service as a volunteer from seventeen. The roll of the obligated is large; how many are actually incorporated is decided by need.

        🇨🇳How does China mark Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper?

        China writes defence into the chapter of the constitution that lists citizens' duties. Article 55 is two sentences in two different registers. The first: to defend the motherland and resist aggression is the sacred duty of every citizen of the People's Republic of China. The second: it is the honourable obligation of citizens to perform military service and join the militia in accordance with law. The first sentence carries no limit of sex or age and prescribes no particular act; only the second lands on institutions, and it puts the militia alongside the armed forces — the vehicle of defence is not only the army. Calling the obligation honourable rather than compulsory is the clearest tonal difference from Article 1 of Taiwan's Military Service Act, which simply states the duty: one writes it as an honour, the other as a legal liability. The actual call-up, the ages and the length of service are left to the Military Service Law; the constitution supplies the place and the name of the duty.

        🇮🇩How does Indonesia mark Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper?

        Article 30(1) of Indonesia's 1945 constitution is worded unusually: every citizen has the right and the duty to take part in the defence and security of the state. Putting a right and a duty in the same sentence is unique among these seven constitutions. Clause (2) then supplies the method — defence and security are carried out through the total people's defence and security system, with the Indonesian National Armed Forces and the National Police as the main force and the people as the supporting force; clause (3) adds that the armed forces consist of army, navy and air force. The design, in other words, is not to draft individuals into an army but to place the whole population in a supporting role. That sets it apart from the Taiwanese and Chinese arrangements, which turn on ages and terms of service: there is no conscription and no stretch of barracks time everyone must pass through, yet the constitution writes every person into the system. The forces themselves are filled by voluntary recruitment, and defence education runs on a separate track.

        🇮🇳How does India mark Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper?

        India has no conscription; its armed forces are entirely volunteer. Yet the duty to defend is written into the constitution. Among the Fundamental Duties listed in Article 51A, clause (d) reads: it shall be the duty of every citizen of India to defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so. The words “when called upon” are the hinge: the duty is conditional, and the condition has never been triggered. Unlike the Directive Principles, which Article 37 says no court may enforce, the Fundamental Duties are a whole Part added in 1976, and they too arrive without an accompanying sanction. India therefore represents a third form among these seven markets: neither turning service into an institution, as Taiwan and China do, nor declining to maintain forces at all, as Japan does, but stating the duty plainly and leaving blank the condition that would activate it. Recruitment is consequently part of the labour market rather than a stage of citizenship.

        🇯🇵How does Japan mark Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper?

        Japan has no conscription, and the reason lies less in policy than in the text of the constitution itself. Article 9 says the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as means of settling international disputes, and then: “In order to accomplish the aim of the preceding paragraph, land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained. The right of belligerency of the state will not be recognized.” Where the other six markets write down who serves and for how long, Japan's starting point is what shall not be maintained at all. Defence in practice is carried by the Self-Defense Forces under their own statute, and its members are recruited by application — the state does not call the individual; the individual applies. No article of the constitution imposes a duty of military service on citizens, which sets Japan apart from China, Indonesia and India as well.

        🇹🇼How does Taiwan mark Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper?

        Article 1 of Taiwan's Military Service Act is one sentence: every male citizen of the Republic of China has, by law, the duty to perform military service. Article 3 frames the years — liability begins on 1 January of the year after a man turns eighteen and ends on 31 December of the year he turns thirty-six. Article 16 is the one most discussed lately: active service for conscripts of eligible age lasts one year, after which they are discharged; the alternative path is military training of up to four months. The same article carries an unusual device: military training or national-defence education courses passed at senior high school or above may be converted at eight class periods to one day of service, up to thirty days off active service and fifteen off training — hours spent in a classroom trade against days spent in barracks. Article 2 divides service into officer, non-commissioned, enlisted and substitute service, and substitute service is Taiwan's ordinary channel for conscience and fitness questions rather than an exceptional clause.

        🇺🇸How does United States mark Conscripted, registered, or merely obliged on paper?

        The United States has not actually drafted anyone since 1973, but the obligation was never abolished — it was narrowed to a single act. 50 U.S.C. §3802 says that, except as otherwise provided, it shall be the duty of every male citizen aged between eighteen and twenty-six on the days fixed for registration, and of every other male person residing in the United States, to present himself for and submit to registration at the times, places and manner set by presidential proclamation and the rules made under it; aliens lawfully admitted as nonimmigrants are excluded while they keep that status. The verb of the whole section is register, not serve. That makes the United States the only one of these seven markets where the obligation stops at a list: the roll keeps being updated, but the switch for a call-up has to be thrown separately by Congress. Failing to register is not only a criminal matter — it is wired to student aid, federal jobs and, in some states, a driver's licence, so the sanction is administrative and scattered across other counters.
